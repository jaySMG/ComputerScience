# CS-255
The DriverPass project involved designing a system for a client named Liam, the owner of a new driving training company called DriverPass. The goal of the system was to allow customers to register, schedule driving lessons, take online classes and practice tests, and track their progress. Additionally, the system needed to manage employees, drivers, vehicles, and packages, while providing reporting tools and integrating DMV updates to ensure compliance with current regulations.

One aspect of my work that I think I did particularly well was creating the UML diagrams—use case, activity, sequence, and class diagrams. These diagrams clearly captured the interactions between the different actors, the flow of activities, and the system structure. I focused on representing all of the client’s requirements, such as lesson scheduling, package management, and online class tracking, in a visual and organized way.

If I could revise one part of my work, I would choose the UML class diagram. While it effectively included all relevant classes and attributes, I would improve it by adding more explicit relationships for future expansion, such as dependencies between OnlineClass and DrivingLesson or optional associations for additional features like notifications and analytics dashboards. This would make the system more flexible and easier to maintain as new features are added.

In designing the system, I interpreted the user’s needs by carefully analyzing the interview transcript and identifying the core actors, their tasks, and the business rules. For example, I incorporated user roles (customer, secretary, driver, administrator, and owner), different lesson packages, online classes, and activity tracking. Considering the user’s needs is critical because it ensures that the system is both usable and effective. A design that doesn’t reflect real-world requirements can lead to wasted development time, poor user adoption, and operational errors.

My approach to designing software begins with requirement analysis—understanding what the client and users need. From there, I use UML diagrams and other modeling tools to structure the system logically, capture relationships, and visualize workflows. In the future, I would also integrate iterative feedback loops with clients, conduct usability testing early in the design phase, and use modular designs to make the system scalable and adaptable. Techniques like scenario analysis, prototyping, and user story mapping would further help in analyzing and designing systems that meet both functional and non-functional requirements.

The app I developed was an inventory management app designed to make tracking items simple and organized. The main goal was to give users an easy way to keep track of their inventory without having to rely on messy spreadsheets or handwritten lists. This app was especially useful for small business owners or anyone who needs to manage multiple items and quantities in one place. It focused on solving the problem of keeping inventory accurate, up to date, and easy to access.

To meet those needs, I built the app with a few key features that make it practical and user-friendly. The main screen displays a list of all inventory items, so users can quickly see what they have. There’s also a feature to add new items, where users can input details like the item name and quantity. I included edit and delete options as well, so users can easily update information or remove items they no longer need. When designing the UI, I tried to keep everything clean and straightforward, with clearly labeled buttons and a layout that doesn’t feel overwhelming. The goal was to make it so that someone could open the app and understand how to use it right away without needing instructions, which I think made the design successful.

Overall, I think I did especially well with the core functionality of the app, specifically the ability to add, update, and delete inventory items.
